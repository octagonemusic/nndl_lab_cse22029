Model Selection and Performance Analysis for Classification of Custom CNN Features Dataset

After a thorough analysis of multiple classification models on the given dataset containing 3253 records and 200 attributes across three class labels, Random Forest Classifier emerges as the most suitable model for this task. This decision is supported by its superior performance metrics, balanced trade-offs, and robust generalization capabilities.  

First, Random Forest achieved the highest test accuracy of 93.86%, indicating its strong predictive performance. Additionally, it excelled in other critical performance measures, with the highest precision (94.07%), F1-score (93.86%), and AUROC (0.9825) among all models tested. These metrics highlight its ability to classify the dataset effectively while maintaining a low rate of false positives and a high balance between precision and recall. AUROC, which assesses the model’s ability to distinguish between classes, further underscores Random Forest’s capability to provide reliable predictions.  

When comparing against alternative models, Random Forest consistently outperformed its competitors. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), while achieving competitive accuracy (93.73%), had slower prediction times and slightly lower precision. Support Vector Machines (SVM), known for strong boundary detection, delivered a lower accuracy of 93.00% and significantly slower prediction speed. Decision Tree, though simple and interpretable, exhibited more overfitting with a test accuracy of only 91.89%, highlighting its limitations in generalization. Naïve Bayes, while computationally efficient, fell short with an accuracy of 92.26% and lower AUROC performance. Random Forest’s ensemble approach, which combines the output of multiple decision trees to enhance predictive power and reduce variance, provided a clear advantage over these simpler models.  

In terms of timing performance, Random Forest required the longest training time of 0.99 seconds, reflecting the computational complexity involved in constructing multiple trees. However, the fast prediction times (0.0197 seconds for test data) are a significant benefit in practical deployment scenarios, where prediction speed is critical. This makes the model highly suitable for real-time applications, as training is a one-time computational cost, whereas predictions are made repeatedly.  

While Random Forest showed some degree of overfitting, indicated by a 6.14% difference between training and test accuracies, this issue can be effectively mitigated through hyperparameter tuning. Techniques such as limiting tree depth, increasing the minimum number of samples per leaf, and applying cross-validation can improve the model’s generalization. Further enhancements, such as feature selection to reduce dimensionality, could also optimize performance by focusing on the most relevant attributes.  

Despite the trade-offs, including higher memory consumption and training time, Random Forest offers a well-rounded solution with a balance of precision, recall, and speed that justifies its computational demands. Its strong performance across multiple evaluation criteria makes it the most robust choice for handling the complexity of the given dataset with 200 features and three output classes.  

In conclusion, Random Forest Classifier stands out as the best model for this classification problem, providing the most reliable, generalizable, and efficient solution. Its high accuracy, superior AUROC, and manageable prediction times ensure that it meets the critical requirements of both performance and practicality. By addressing overfitting through careful parameter tuning, this model can be further optimized, reinforcing its suitability for real-world deployment.
